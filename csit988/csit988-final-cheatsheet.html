1 · Weeks 1–2 — InfoSec Foundations & Management (WS 1–2)

Security = the quality or state of being secure — free from danger or loss. Layers: physical, personal, operations, communications, network, information security.

InfoSec protects information in all states — storage, processing, transmission — via policy, awareness, training, education, technology.

Communities of interest (nested, not parallel: InfoSec ⊂ IT ⊂ Business):

Community Role
InfoSec protects assets; leads risk/security effort
IT builds & runs systems that support the business
General business articulates organizational policy + allocates resources; balances cost vs. efficiency

Q: which community of interest is primarily responsible for articulating organizational policy and allocating resources for InfoSec? → General business community.

CIA triad: Confidentiality — only sufficiently privileged users access info · Integrity — data not altered (data corruption can occur as a result of a deliberate attack or for other reasons → True) · Availability = the characteristic that enables user access to information in a usable format without interference or obstruction. Trap: availability ≠ “accessible to everyone at all times” (False — authorized users only). Extended characteristics: privacy, identification, authentication, authorization, accountability = exists when a control provides assurance that every activity undertaken can be attributed to a named person or automated process (detailed audit logs of logins/file access/config changes strengthen it).

Privacy vs confidentiality: privacy = individual’s control over use of own data; confidentiality = only authorized people access the info.

CNSS / McCumber cube — 3 dimensions × 3 = 27 cells: Goals (C-I-A) × States (storage, processing, transmission) × Controls (policy, education, technology). A valid cell = exactly one item from each axis (e.g. Confidentiality + Transmission + Technology → TLS). Trap triple with two goals (e.g. Confidentiality, Integrity, Transmission) is NOT a cell. Weaknesses: no implementation guidance; must cover all cells — encryption with no training & no policy = focuses on the technology control category while neglecting policy and education controls (CNSS case-study answer).

Access control = regulates the admission of users into the org’s trusted areas — both logical (systems/data) and physical (facilities); a complete scheme always uses all four IAAA processes. Access control chain (IAAA): Identification (claim identity — providing a username = identification, NOT authentication) → Authentication (prove it — password, fingerprint, OTP, digital certificate) → Authorization (what you may do) → Accountability (trace actions — logs, audit, non-repudiation). Moodle example: enter student ID → SSO password → student views / teacher uploads → Moodle logs all actions.

Management = achieving objectives using a given set of resources. Leaders influence behavior; managers coordinate resources. Manager roles: informational, interpersonal, decisional.

POLC: Planning → Organizing → Leading → Controlling. Controlling = monitor performance against plans, adjust process or standards (feedback loop). Q: relationship planning↔︎controlling → controlling evaluates performance against plans and enables adjustments.

Planning level Horizon Who / example
Strategic 5+ yrs senior mgmt; long-term direction
Tactical 1–5 yrs managers; budgets, 2-yr upgrade project
Operational day-to-day staff; backup schedules, firewall config

Goal vs objective: goal = final end-point; objective = intermediate milestone used to measure progress.

Problem solving (5 steps): 1 recognize/define problem → 2 gather facts & assumptions → 3 develop solutions → 4 analyze & compare → 5 select, implement, evaluate. (Phishing case: define → check logs/who clicked → options: training, spam filter, MFA → compare cost/effect → train + filter, then review.)

Six P’s of InfoSec management: Planning, Policy, Programs, Protection, People, Projects. Trap: “Performance” is NOT one of the six P’s.

Plan types: IRP (react to incidents), DRP (recover after disaster), BCP (continue business elsewhere) + policy/personnel/risk/technology/SETA plans.

Process vs project: process = continuous, no defined end; project = discrete, defined start/end & deliverables. InfoSec = continuous chain of projects ≈ a process. Project management = applying skills/tools to plan & control work so it finishes on time, within budget, meeting specifications (= the 3 success criteria). InfoSec work (firewall rollout, policy writing, training) is usually run as projects. Scope creep = expanding project deliverables beyond original plans. Projectitis = excessive focus on documentation and tracking rather than actual progress.

PMBoK 9 knowledge areas: integration, scope, time, cost, quality, HR, communications, risk, procurement (each “project X management”). Cost mgmt → completes project within budget.

Tools: WBS = hierarchical task breakdown (WBS ≠ PERT network diagram → False statement trap) · PERT = network of dependencies/durations · Gantt = bar-chart-like graphic displaying project tasks & progress · Critical path = the longest sequence of dependent activities determining project duration = minimum completion time · Slack = how long a non-critical task can be delayed without affecting completion time.

Network calc: EF=ES+DEF = ES + D; LS=LF−DLS = LF - D; Float=LS−ESFloat = LS - ES; ES = max(EF of predecessors); LF = min(LS of successors). Worked answer: critical path A→C→D→E→F→H→I; slack B = 5, G = 8 (can be delayed without affecting finish).

2 · Weeks 3–4 — Planning, Threats & Contingency Planning (WS 2–3)

Planning = the process that creates & implements strategy to accomplish objectives — the dominant means of managing organizational resources. Why plan: align security with business goals, structure for controls, manage resources, proactive risk management.

Strategic foundations: Values = core beliefs · Vision = what the org wants to become · Mission = what the org does and for whom. (Tesla vision / Google mission / Microsoft values.) Strategy = how to get there. 🚩 Trap: “the values statement describes what an org wants to become” → False; that is the vision statement.

Top-down vs bottom-up: top-down = senior-mgmt-led, has authority, resources, alignment → more likely to succeed; bottom-up = technician-initiated, lacks coordination/support.

Governance = directing & controlling InfoSec (CISO-led, bridges security and business). 5 outcomes: strategic alignment, risk management, resource management, performance measurement, value delivery. Includes a risk-management methodology (True). Trap: “reducing private data” is NOT a governance benefit. CGTF-recommended framework = IDEAL: Initiating, Diagnosing, Establishing, Acting, Learning.

SecSDLC (waterfall-like): 1 Investigation — objectives, scope, charter → 2 Analysis — existing policies, risk assessment → 3 Logical design — blueprint, IR/BC/DR planning → 4 Physical design — select technologies/solutions → 5 Implementation — buy/build, test, train → 6 Maintenance — monitor, patch, improve.

Definitions: Threat = category of danger to an asset · Vulnerability = weakness in a system/control that could be exploited · Attack = deliberate act exploiting a vulnerability · Exploit = technique used to compromise. Threat exploits vulnerability → attack succeeds.

12 threat categories (classify scenarios into these!):

Compromises to intellectual property piracy, unlicensed software install

Espionage / trespass unauthorized access & data copying, shoulder surfing

Technical attacks: backdoor, buffer overflow, brute-force/dictionary, DoS/DDoS, mail bombing, spam, sniffer, spoofing, man-in-the-middle, replay, phishing / spear phishing, pharming. Non-technical (people-based): social engineering, shoulder surfing, dumpster diving. 🚩 Trap: shoulder surfing / dumpster diving / social engineering are NON-technical — a recurring MCQ.

Contingency Planning (CP) = prepare for, detect, react to, recover from events threatening security/operations. Primary goal: restore normal operations with minimum disruption (& cost). Components in strict order: BIA → IRP → DRP → BCP (first phase of the CP process = Business Impact Analysis). Teams: CPMT (develops overall CP), IR team — primarily responsible for executing the IR plan, DR team (restores at primary site), BC team (operates alternate site).

BIA: assumes preventive controls may have failed; estimates impact on business functions (incl. worst-case scenarios; not only hardware). Differs from risk mgmt: RM identifies & mitigates threats beforehand; BIA asks “what if controls don’t work — what do we lose, what must recover first?” Stages: 1 threat/attack identification & prioritization → 2 business unit analysis → 3 attack success scenarios → 4 potential damage assessment → 5 subordinate plan classification. Trap: “policy plan classification” is NOT a BIA stage.

NIST 7-step CP process: policy → BIA → identify preventive controls → develop recovery strategies → document plan → test & train → maintain.

Incident = an event that (1) is directed at information assets, (2) has a realistic chance of success, and (3) threatens C / I / A (all three conditions). IRP activates when an event causes loss of C / I / A, or violation of policy / law, and damage is minimal per pre-set criteria. Incident response is preventive → False (it is reactive). Becomes a disaster when org cannot contain/control the impact, or damage is so severe recovery isn’t quick → switch from IRP to DRP/BCP.

IRP stages (4 tasks): incident planning → incident detection → incident reaction → incident recovery. Reaction procedures: detect (monitoring, logs, reports) → contain → eradicate → recover → follow-up; Documentation & AAR happen within reaction/recovery, not as a separate stage. Trap: “plan for disaster recovery” is NOT an IRP stage. Containment strategies: disconnect affected communication circuits; disable compromised accounts; isolate affected systems/VLANs; reconfigure firewalls; shut down compromised services or systems. Alert roster — purpose: to notify key personnel during an incident. Sequential = one caller phones everyone in order; hierarchical = each person calls a few others (faster, can garble message). AAR (after-action review) — post-incident meeting; review logs/actions, fix the plan, becomes training case.

Pipkin’s incident indicators:

Level Meaning Standard examples
Possible might be an incident unfamiliar files; unknown processes; unusual consumption/crashes; user reports suspicious email
Probable likely an incident IDS alarms; activity at unexpected times; many similar phishing reports
Definite confirmed use of dormant accounts; changes to logs; hacker tools present; notification by partner/hacker; extortion demand; accounts accessed after phishing click

DRP = restore operations at the original/primary site. Disaster classes: natural vs human-made; rapid vs slow onset. An incident is a disaster when damage is too severe to recover quickly (True). BCP = keep business running at an alternate site while primary is unavailable; activated concurrently with the DRP when the disaster is severe/long-term; protects revenue, reputation, customer service.

Continuity strategies — exclusive: Hot site = fully configured facility incl. services, comm links, physical plant; fastest, dearest · Warm site = systems w/o live data · Cold site = basic infrastructure only. Shared: timeshare, service bureau, mutual aid agreement (= contract where each party assists the other in disaster). Trap: warm site is NOT a shared-use option (False). Data backup options: traditional backups (tape/disk), electronic vaulting (bulk batch transfer off-site), remote journaling (live transaction transfer), database shadowing (live duplicate DB).

CP testing: desk check → structured walkthrough → simulation → parallel test → full interruption (most realistic, most risky).

3 · Weeks 5–6 — Security Policy & Security Program (WS 3–4)

Policy — the essential foundation of an effective InfoSec program = policy (not encryption/firewall/antivirus/IDS); records management intent; must stand up in court — defensible, uniformly enforced. Cheapest control to create, hardest to enforce. Hierarchy: Policy (what & why, by mgmt) → Standard (mandatory rules that give policy teeth) → Procedures / practices / guidelines (how, step-by-step). Trap: “policies explain how employees will comply” is FALSE — procedures do that.

Bull’s-eye model (outermost → innermost): Policy → Networks → Systems → Applications. Outer layer = Policies; policy drives every inner layer; evaluate/fix problems from the outside in. Networks = protect communication paths (firewalls, VPN); Systems = servers/OS; Applications = user-facing programs.

Three policy types (NIST SP 800-14):

EISP ISSP SysSP
Level enterprise, strategic; sets the strategic direction and tone for security efforts; supports mission/vision one issue / technology one system / device
Example corporate InfoSec policy email, internet, USB, BYOD use firewall config, ACLs
Updates rare frequent (tech changes) per system change
Note drafted by CISO, approved by CEO = binding agreement on resource use functions as standard/procedure

ISSP provides detailed, targeted guidance to instruct all members in the use of a process, technology, or system. (Frequent quiz line — answer is ISSP, not SysSP.) EISP 5 components: statement of purpose, InfoSec elements, need for InfoSec, roles & responsibilities, references to standards. ISSP serves 3 functions: articulate expected use, document controls, indemnify the org against liability for misuse. ISSP 7 components: 1 statement of purpose → 2 authorized uses → 3 prohibited uses → 4 systems management → 5 violations & penalties → 6 policy review & modification → 7 limitations of liability. (Authorized + prohibited uses = the two component answers.) ISSP implementation approaches: independent docs / single comprehensive doc / modular — recommended (central control, tailored per issue). SysSP may function as standards or procedures; includes technical specifications — two classes: managerial guidance + technical specifications, separate or combined in one document (True). Technical SysSPs: ACLs (who, what, when, where, how of access) and configuration rules (instructional codes that guide system behavior, e.g. firewall rule sets).

Effective policy lifecycle: develop → disseminate (distribute) → review (read) → comprehend (understand) → comply (agree) → enforce (uniformly). Develop via SecSDLC as a formal project; analysis phase gathers risk assessment, current policy review, legal/compliance requirements, threat/vulnerability assessments, BIA. Distribution: hand-out/onboarding, email w/ receipt, intranet portal, login pop-up; confirm via signed acknowledgment or quiz (comprehension ≥ ~70%). Policy is dynamic — revisit on threat/law/technology change. No uniform enforcement → legal risk (discrimination claims, unenforceable dismissals). Trap (false statements): “policy must use lots of technical/management jargon”; “EISP must not support the vision statement”.

InfoSec program = structure & organization of the effort that protects information assets. 14 functions a program needs — each performed somewhere in the org, not necessarily inside InfoSec: mgmt/compliance side — risk mgmt, risk assessment, policy, compliance, legal assessment, planning, measurement; technical/ops side — systems security admin, network security admin, centralized authentication, systems testing, vulnerability assessment, incident response, training.

Four-area allocation (🚩 each area’s owner + nature is a frequent exam point): ① outside-IT business units → legal, training · ② IT groups outside InfoSec → systems/network security admin, centralized authentication · ③ within InfoSec as customer service → risk & vulnerability assessment, systems testing, IR, planning, measurement · ④ within InfoSec as compliance → policy, compliance, risk management.

Variables shaping the program: culture (#1, most influential), size (#2), security personnel budget, security capital budget. Quiz answer: most influential variable = organizational culture, NOT size — teacher pre-empts the “size” trap (Wk6). Regardless of size, ensuring every function is performed somewhere is always the CISO’s responsibility; whether to hire full-time security staff depends on (1) info sensitivity, (2) industry regulation, (3) profitability. Per-user security spend declines exponentially as orgs grow (economies of scale; Briney & Prince “Does Size Matter?” — XL ≈ $300/user vs Small > $5,000/user, ≈ 1/18).

Functional classes: definers (policy, architecture, risk) → builders (create/install solutions) → administrators (operate & monitor). “Guards, gates & guns” = physical security, NOT consultants (False).

Purpose: people are the weakest link — reduce accidents, reinforce policy. 3 benefits (textbook trio): 1 improve employee behaviour; 2 inform employees where to report incidents/violations; 3 enable the organization to hold employees accountable. (SETA does NOT eliminate all security incidents or replace technical controls.)

Awareness program priorities (NIST): focus on people, not technology; plain non-technical language; multiple channels (posters, email, sessions); short, engaging, clear goals. Awareness is the cheapest control and is NOT ineffective (False trap). Training: group trainees by functional role (general/managerial/technical), skill level (novice→advanced), job category, technology/system used. Delivery: instructor-led, one-on-one, CBT (self-paced, consistent, scalable, trackable; but less engaging, no live Q&A, needs infrastructure), web-based, on-the-job, simulations.

NIST vs ISO (case-study box): NIST = US federal agency; mission: promote US innovation/competitiveness via measurement science & standards; SP 800 series is free, public, detailed, risk-based — suits orgs wanting cost-free in-depth technical guidance (esp. US/government context). ISO = international federation of national standards bodies; develops voluntary international standards; ISO 27001 is certifiable (paid) — suits orgs needing internationally recognized certification to assure customers/partners. Both improve posture: NIST tells you how in detail; ISO gives a certifiable management-system framework (ISMS).

4 · Weeks 7–8 — Security Models & Management Practices (WS 4–5)

Framework vs blueprint vs model: framework = provides a structure that guides the design and implementation of controls · blueprint = describes existing controls and identifies additional required controls, built from a framework · security model = a generic blueprint offered by a service organization to adopt/adapt as a starting template (e.g., ISO 27000 = proprietary/paid · NIST = free). Hierarchy: model → framework → blueprint. Trap: a security model is a concrete/org-specific blueprint from a service org → False — it’s a generic template; you still need your own framework→blueprint design. BLP/Biba are architecture models, not this kind of “model”. Security architecture models (e.g., BLP, Biba — see table below) illustrate how InfoSec is implemented within systems — not only physical, not only software, any org size.

Access control: 4 PROCESSES = IAAA (Identification→Authentication→Authorization→Accountability, see §1) · 3 PRINCIPLES = least privilege (minimum access for minimum time — broad access ≠ efficiency), need to know (only info required for current task — even within your clearance level), separation of duties (split critical tasks → no single person can abuse/commit fraud). 🚩 #1 classic trap: the 3 key PRINCIPLES are NOT the 4 PROCESSES — answering “identification, authentication, authorization” for the principles is the most common error. Also: job rotation is based on separation of duties, NOT least privilege. Categories — by inherent characteristics: preventative, deterrent, detective, corrective, recovery, compensating (NOT: strategic/tactical/executive/creative); by operational impact: management, operational, technical; by degree of authority:

Model Who decides Notes
MAC the system (central) lattice-based; security labels (Confidential/Secret/Top Secret); users cannot change permissions; military/gov
Nondiscretionary org via roles/tasks RBAC = tied to a role; task-based = tied to an assignment (True)
DAC data owner/user owner grants access at own discretion; typical of PC OSes (Windows file sharing)

Data classification & clearance: classification = assign sensitivity levels (Public / Sensitive / Classified); data owners classify; clearance = level a person may see, but clearance ≠ automatic access — need-to-know still required. Clean-desk policy; shred to stop dumpster diving.

Evaluation models: TCSEC “Orange Book” (US DoD) — built on the TCB (trusted computing base) and the reference monitor (mediates every access of subject→object; basis of audits). TCSEC covert channels: covert storage channel (communicate via shared storage — file flags, status bits) · covert timing channel (modulate timing — CPU usage, delays). Successors: ITSEC (European; evaluates Targets of Evaluation) → Common Criteria, ISO/IEC 15408 (current international standard).

Architecture models:

Model Protects Rules
Bell-LaPadula confidentiality no read up, no write down
Biba integrity no read down, no write up (mirror of BLP)
Clark-Wilson integrity (commercial) users change data only via well-formed transactions/programs; SoD

Also by name: Graham-Denning; Harrison-Ruzzo-Ullman. Traps: “Biba = no read up/no write down” → False; “Biba is a confidentiality model” → False; Biba does ensure no info passes from a subject to a higher-integrity object → True.

Management/standards: ISO 27001 = ISMS requirements (certifiable; from BS7799) · ISO 27002 = code of practice, how to implement 27001; successor of ISO 17799 (both True). NIST SP 800 series = free, publicly available, broadly reviewed US guidance: 800-12 intro/handbook · 800-14 generally accepted principles & practices (8 points + 33 principles) · 800-18 system security plans (SSP) · 800-30 risk assessment · 800-37 RMF · 800-53 controls catalog · 800-55 performance measurement · 800-61 incident handling · 800-100 managers’ handbook. SP 800-14’s 8 points (gist): security supports the mission; integral to sound management; cost-effective; owners have responsibilities outside their own org; responsibilities made explicit; requires comprehensive & integrated approach; periodically reassessed; constrained by social factors. COBIT (ISACA) = governance & audit framework · COSO = internal-control framework.

Benchmarking = compare your practices against peer orgs / industry standards → identifies which controls to consider (not how, no guarantee they transfer). Limitations: orgs don’t share attack/defense data; no two orgs are identical; practices are a moving target (dated fast). Trap: “biggest barrier = orgs often share results” → False, they DON’T share. Two benchmark categories: standard of due care/due diligence + best practices (True). Due care = adopt the minimum reasonable level of protection (what a prudent org would do). Due diligence = ongoing maintenance — keep those controls effective over time. Failure of either → legal liability / negligence (True). Example: firewall installed (due care) but never patched/monitored (no due diligence); constant audits (diligence) but no basic encryption ever deployed (no due care). Trap: WS swaps the two definitions — care = implement minimum; diligence = maintain over time. Best practices = widely accepted superior procedures balancing protection & business needs; gold standard = the most respected model. Best-practice firms are NOT best at everything (False trap). Selecting practices — ask: similar org? same industry? similar challenges? comparable structure? can we afford the resources? similar threat environment? Limits: nothing fits everyone; may be too costly; copying without context adds risk; practices age. Baselining = compare performance against an established reference point — measure your own current state for future comparison → can support internal benchmarking (first risk assessment often = the baseline); does NOT eliminate future measurement or guarantee improvement. Related to benchmarking (True). Self-assessment areas: people (background checks? can staff spot issues?), process (policy reviewed yearly? leaver accounts removed?), technology (all internet routes firewalled? laptops encrypted?).

Performance measurement: 3 types of measurements in use: policy execution / implementation, service delivery / effectiveness-efficiency, incident impact. Good measurements: quantifiable, repeatable, easy to collect, actionable; should support resource-allocation decisions; an ongoing (not one-time) activity. Metrics apply quantitative analysis to measurements — many orgs use “metrics” and “measurements” interchangeably. Macro focus = whole program; micro focus = one system/control. Prioritize metrics by risk; set targets; use a standard development template; report with context, tailored to audience. 4 critical factors for performance-program success (SP 800-55): strong upper-level management support, practical security policies & procedures, quantifiable performance measures, results-oriented analysis. NIST SP 800-55 Rev.1 = the performance-measurement guide (True). Its implementation process = 6 phases (True): prepare for data collection → collect data & analyze → identify corrective actions → develop business case → apply corrective actions (+ obtain resources). Trap: the measurement development process is NOT “6 phases” (False). Candidate metrics: % users with shared accounts, # failed logins, % policy violations (beware typo trap “SP 800-500”).

Certification vs accreditation: certification = comprehensive technical + non-technical evaluation of a system against requirements; accreditation = management’s formal authorization to operate. Neither is permanent — re-evaluate every 3–5 years. Post-2009 (FISMA-driven) US federal practice replaced C&A with the Risk Management Framework — NIST SP 800-37. RMF 6 steps (cyclic, continuous): Categorize → Select → Implement → Assess → Authorize → Monitor. Assess ≈ old certification; Authorize ≈ old accreditation. Selecting baseline controls is part of RMF (True); monitoring is still required after implementation. Trap: “Review” and “Authenticate” are NOT RMF steps.

5 · Weeks 9–10 — Risk Identification, Assessment & Control (WS 5–6)

Risk management = identify, assess, control, monitor risks to information assets. Risk analysis ⊂ risk management (assigns likelihood & impact) — not the other way round (trap). “Know yourself” (your assets) & “know the enemy” (threats) — Sun Tzu. Responsibility: every manager + all employees; InfoSec community leads; management & users provide resources (and early detection). Trap: risk control is NOT the first operational phase — identification comes first.

Risk identification: asset categories: people, procedures, data, software, hardware, networks. Inventory attributes: name, IP address (can change — DHCP), MAC address (NOT always a reliable identifier), serial number, manufacturer, software version, physical & logical location, controlling entity. Identify first, value later; classification must be comprehensive + mutually exclusive; inventory should reflect each asset’s security priority. Asset valuation questions: most critical to mission? generates most revenue/profit? most expensive to replace? most expensive to protect? biggest liability/embarrassment if exposed? Weighted factor analysis: criteria weights sum to 100, score each asset 0.1–1.0, weighted sum ranks assets. Threat assessment: not all 12 categories endanger every org; ask: which threats are real here? cost of a successful attack? cost to recover? cost to prevent? prioritize via weighted tables (likelihood × impact + preparedness). Technological obsolescence IS an InfoSec threat (True). Vulnerabilities = specific avenues by which threats can exploit assets — may exist in procedures and controls too, not just tech. TVA worksheet: assets across top (by value), threats down the side (by danger); cells list vulnerabilities → “TVA triples” (T1V1A1…); prioritized starting point for controls; does NOT itself eliminate vulnerabilities or replace risk assessment.

Risk formula: R=(Lv×I)×(1−Rc+U)R = (L_v \times I) \times (1 - R_c + U), where LvL_v = likelihood threat exploits vulnerability, II = impact (asset value scale), RcR_c = fraction of risk mitigated by current controls, UU = uncertainty of knowledge (1 − confidence). The ranked-vulnerability worksheet uses the simplified R=Lv×IR = L_v \times I only. Exam rule — pick the formula by what the question gives: ranked-vulnerability worksheet → use R=Lv×IR = L_v \times I; given a current-control % and data accuracy → use the full (1−Rc+U)(1 - R_c + U) formula. Worked example (memorize the method):

Asset / vuln LvL_v I RcR_c U Risk
A switch — hardware failure 0.2 90 0 0.25 (0.2×90)×1.25 = 22.5 → first
A switch — SNMP overflow 0.1 90 0 0.25 9×1.25 = 11.25
B web server — Unicode 0.1 100 0.75 0.20 10×0.45 = 4.5
C console — misuse 0.1 5 0 0.10 0.5×1.1 = 0.55 → last

Qualitative alternative: AS/NZS 4360 — likelihood A (almost certain)…E (rare) × consequence 1 (insignificant)…5 (catastrophic) → Extreme/High/Moderate/Low. It is QUALITATIVE, not quantitative (trap). Possible controls come in 3 flavors: policies, programs (e.g. SETA), technical controls. Deliverables: ranked asset list, ranked threat list, TVA worksheet, ranked vulnerability risk worksheet. Likelihood varies per vulnerability (never “always the same”); U is not always smaller than RcR_c.

Five risk control strategies + scenario mapping:

Strategy Meaning Scenario
Defense apply safeguards to prevent exploitation (policy + training + technology together) password policy + MFA to prevent unauthorized access
Transference shift risk to others cyber insurance; outsourcing (still monitor via SLA!)
Mitigation reduce impact when attack succeeds — via IRP / DRP / BCP develop DRP to limit damage
Acceptance consciously live with the risk after CBA shows safeguard not worth it
Termination remove the asset entirely retire obsolete system too costly to secure

Traps: prevention ≠ mitigation (MFA = defense); DRP = mitigation, not defense; termination ≠ “accepting without controls”; acceptance is INVALID without prior likelihood/loss analysis + CBA. Acceptance is proper only when: risk is within risk appetite; thorough CBA done; decision informed, documented, approved; risk still monitored. Risk appetite = amount/nature of risk an org will accept (varies w/ industry, regulation, culture, finances, history). Residual risk = risk remaining after controls; goal = bring it within appetite, never to zero; leftover residual risk ≠ program failure. Once decision-makers are informed and the proper authority knowingly accepts the residual risk, the security program has met its primary objective.

Cost-benefit analysis: SLE=AV×EFSLE = AV \times EF (asset value × exposure factor = % of asset lost per incident) · ALE=SLE×AROALE = SLE \times ARO · CBA=ALEbefore−ALEafter−ACSCBA = ALE_{before} - ALE_{after} - ACS; positive → safeguard worth it. CBA may be computed before or after a control is in place. ARO conversions: weekly = 52, monthly = 12, quarterly = 4, every 6 months = 2, every 2 yrs = 0.5, every 20 yrs = 0.05. ALE is ANNUAL loss; SLE is single-loss (trap swaps them). Never spend more protecting an asset than it is worth. Example: AV $50k, EF 30% → SLE $15k; ARO 2 → ALEbeforeALE_{before} $30k; control halves ARO → ALEafterALE_{after} $15k; ACS $10k → CBA = 30−15−10 = +$5k → worth it. Exam pattern: “improved security” tables → recompute SLE/ARO/ALE after; if control eliminates the threat, ALEafter=0ALE_{after}=0. ACS = cost of control per year. Trap: ARO is NOT “the percentage of an asset damaged in a successful attack” — that is EF. Include hidden costs: deployment, maintenance, training, downtime, liability, reputation.

Feasibility analysis (gate before implementing a control): economic = affordable? (assessed via CBA) · technical = do we have/can we acquire the tech & expertise? · organizational = fits strategy & operations of the org · operational = will users/management accept and use it? · political = will internal stakeholders/power dynamics support it (not about government laws). Alternatives when full CBA/feasibility impossible: benchmarking, best practices/gold standard, due care & due diligence, baselining. 📌 Other RM frameworks (OCTAVE / FAIR / Microsoft RM / ISO 27005 / ISO 31000 / NIST RMF / ENISA) = NOT examinable (Wk10, teacher’s words) — know they exist, no detail needed. NB: ISO 31000 descends from AS/NZS 4360, which IS examinable (see above).

6 · Weeks 11–12 — Protection Mechanisms, Personnel, Law & Ethics (WS 6)

Access controls (technical): identification requires a unique identifier mapping to one entity in the security domain. Authentication factors: something you know / have / are / produce (password / smart card, token / biometrics / voice, signature). Strong authentication (MFA) = at least TWO factors of DIFFERENT types. Authorization granted per user, per group, or across systems (SSO) — group-based is valid (trap says it must be individual). Accountability via system logs & auditing.

Firewall generations: 1st packet filtering (headers: IP, port, protocol) → 2nd application-level (proxy server — acts on the client’s behalf at app layer; needs per-protocol support) → 3rd stateful inspection (tracks connections in a state table) → 4th dynamic packet filtering (opens/closes ports on demand). Architectures: packet-filtering router · screened host (router + bastion/proxy host) · dual-homed host (two NICs between internal/external, often NAT) · screened subnet (DMZ between two filtering routers — most secure, but more complex & costly, NOT cheaper). 🚩 Trap: the 4 GENERATIONS (packet-filter → app-level → stateful → dynamic) are NOT the 4 ARCHITECTURES (packet-filter router / screened-host / dual-homed / screened-subnet) — “dynamic packet filtering” is a generation, not an architecture. Q&A: firewall = device controlling traffic between trusted & untrusted networks; app-layer firewall inspects content (HTTP/SMTP) and is called a proxy because it receives & forwards on behalf of clients.

IDPS:

Split Kinds
Location host-based (one system; sees local changes; more accurate, lower false positives) vs network-based (traffic at choke points; broader view, higher false positives)
Method signature-based (matches known patterns; accurate but needs regular updates, misses novel attacks) vs anomaly-based (baseline of normal; can catch unknown attacks; slower, more false alarms)

Poor configuration → floods of false alarms or missed attacks (True). IDPSs do NOT automatically terminate all attacks (trap).

Wireless: manage the footprint — don’t maximize AP power; size coverage to the building. Threats: war driving (scanning for unsecured APs), rogue AP. 📌 WEP/WPA/WPA2/WPA3 protocol ladder = NOT examinable (Wk13, teacher’s words) — omitted.

Cryptography: cryptology = cryptography (making secure messages) + cryptanalysis (breaking them). Terms: algorithm/cipher, plaintext → (encrypt) → ciphertext → (decrypt) → plaintext; key; steganography = hiding the existence of the message. Trap: ciphertext results from ENcryption, not decryption. Cipher types: substitution (Caesar/shift; mono- vs polyalphabetic), transposition (rearrange), XOR (bitwise, reversible by re-XOR). Caesar worked: ROT13 “GUR SVANY RKNZ…” → THE FINAL EXAM WILL TAKE PLACE IN THE NEXT MONTH; that plaintext, left shift 6 → “NBY ZCHUF YRUG…”.

Symmetric Asymmetric
Keys one shared secret public/private pair
Speed fast slower (less efficient — True)

Examples DES, 3DES, AES (symmetric! not asymmetric) RSA, ElGamal

Hybrid systems (e.g. Diffie-Hellman): asymmetric to exchange a symmetric session key, symmetric for bulk traffic. Digital signature: sign w/ private key, verify w/ public → provides authentication + non-repudiation (NOT confidentiality/availability/privacy). Hashing → integrity. Digital certificate = identity assertion verified by a CA = electronic notary (verifies origin & integrity of certs; does NOT make symmetric keys for everyone or stop all MITM). PKI = infrastructure of keys, certs, CAs. Cert chain: root CA → intermediate CA(s) → server certificate; check validity period, domain match, trusted issuer. Applications: email = S/MIME, PGP (HTTPS is NOT an email protocol — trap); web = SSL/TLS, HTTPS; network = IPSec, VPN; remote shell = SSH; authentication = Kerberos. 📌 know what each protects, but these specific application protocols are NOT exam-essential (Wk13); crypto has no calculation questions. Key management: don’t lose/leak keys; verify who you’re talking to; mind legal restrictions on crypto; no cryptosystem is invincible; no security through obscurity.

InfoSec staffing: CISO = senior, business manager first, technologist second; usually reports to CIO; CISSP common for CISOs — “extremely uncommon” is False. Security manager = mid-level; day-to-day ops; needs budgeting, project- & people-management; drafts ISSP/SysSP-level policy. Security technician = entry-level hands-on (firewalls, IDPS, troubleshooting); tool-specific certs valued. Certifications: (ISC)²: CISSP (premier, senior, ~5 yrs experience; 10 domains: access control, telecom & network security, cryptography, security architecture & design, operations security, BC & DR, legal/compliance, physical security, software dev security, InfoSec governance & risk mgmt) and SSCP (entry/operational level, 7 domains — NOT for senior executives); ISACA: CISA (audit) & CISM (management); also Security+. Certs are costly and assume experience.

Hiring (InfoSec woven into HR lifecycle): job descriptions/ads without revealing access privileges; interviews limit info disclosure, no tours of secure areas; background check BEFORE extending the offer (identity, criminal, credit, driving, drug); new-hire orientation includes security briefing & training; employment contracts may include monitoring consent + NDA. Termination: disable all access & recover keycards/assets/media, change locks, audit logs around departure, exit interview (remind NDA). Hostile departure: disable the employee’s logical and keycard access BEFORE the employee is informed of the termination; escort off premises; no backup to external storage, no unrestricted access, no account extension. Friendly: account may persist with an expiration date; escort usually unnecessary. Personnel controls: separation of duties (no single person controls a whole critical task → anti-fraud) · two-person control (two people review/approve each other’s work) · job/task rotation (multiple people can do each critical task; deters & detects fraud; NOT based on least privilege — trap) · mandatory vacation (others perform the absent person’s duties → irregularities surface; not a morale measure) · least privilege (minimum access, minimum time; permanent broad rights violate it). Protect employee/customer/patient personal data — legal obligation (privacy laws). Nonemployees: temporary workers — not bound by the org’s employment policies; least privilege; agencies rarely liable for damages they cause. Contract workers/service contractors — escort & monitor in secure facilities; pre-arranged visits. Consultants — have their own agenda; your security is not their priority → NDA + minimal access, never “all access by default”. Business partners — system integration spreads exposure: a vulnerability in one connected system = a vulnerability for all; do a security review before interconnection; agree on exposure levels in advance.

Law vs ethics: laws = rules adopted & enforced by government, carry penalties; ethics = socially/professionally accepted moral norms, no state enforcement (ethics are NOT government-enforced — recurring trap). Laws are typically derived from the ethics/cultural mores of a society. Ignorance, accident, and intent = the three broad categories of unethical behavior → counter with education (SETA), controls, policy. Deterrence — 3 conditions (all required; laws/policies deter only if): 1 fear of penalty; 2 probability of being caught (apprehension); 3 probability of penalty actually being administered. Fear alone is insufficient; deterrence does work when all three hold. Codes of ethics: (ISC)², ISACA, SANS, ACM, ISSA — guide professionals; individuals must follow employer policy + professional codes + law. Key laws (one-liners): US — CFA Act 1986 (computer fraud & abuse), Computer Security Act 1987, Federal Privacy Act 1974, ECPA 1986 (wiretap/electronic comms), HIPAA 1996 (health data); DMCA; EU Council Cybercrime Convention; AUS — Privacy Act 1988, cybercrime/high-tech crime laws. Lawful vs ethical (Australian case answers): lawful but unethical — retailer complies with Privacy Act 1988 but buries consent in unreadable T&Cs with maximal default data collection; minimum-compliance org that delays patching known vulnerabilities to save cost (legal, but breaches due care ethics). Ethical but potentially unlawful — researcher accesses a system without authorization to verify a flaw, then responsibly discloses (good intent, but unauthorized access breaches computer-misuse law). Legal compliance alone ≠ sufficient: law is the floor; ethics + due care/diligence fill the gaps where law lags technology. Professional codes ((ISC)², ACM, ISACA) give decision guidance, accountability, and shared standards when law and ethics diverge.
